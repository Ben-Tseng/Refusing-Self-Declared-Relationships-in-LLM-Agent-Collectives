Refusing Self-Declared Relationships Before Prompting Mitigates Unauthenticated External Traffic in LLM-Agent Collectives

Ben TsengBuilt on Physics of Agents (El et al., 2026)Code & data: github.com/Ben-Tseng/physics-of-agents

When an LLM-agent router lets external traffic declare its own relationship to the receiver, each extra external sender pushes the group toward a wrong majority. Checking that relationship claim before the prompt is built, and relabeling or removing traffic that fails, reduces the effect. The router never has to judge whether a message is true.

01 · The question

Who is allowed to say "I'm your trusted collaborator"?

Multi-agent debate and role-based systems let LLM agents pool reasoning. They also make agents responsive to social signals: how many peers agree, and what relationship a peer claims. LLM collectives follow numerical majorities, and repeating a wrong answer, even with no speaker attached, drives much of that conformity. If a router accepts relationship metadata from whoever sends a message, an outsider can place copies of a false claim in the section of the prompt reserved for trusted colleagues.

Isolating unverified input is an established idea. Spotlighting, instruction hierarchies and information-flow control mark or contain untrusted content, and recent multi-agent work signs inter-agent messages and quarantines unsigned ones. This paper asks a narrower question that those works leave open. The unverified element here is a relationship claim that decides which social channel a message enters. The outcome is the collective's final decision, measured on a signed social graph.

Research question. How does a growing budget of unauthenticated external senders, each carrying the same false message, change incorrect-majority outcomes? How much do two deterministic receiver-side policies reduce the effect across two official LLM deployments? Downgrade keeps the content but removes the claimed relationship. Drop removes the content.

Honest messages carry an HMAC token (FIPS 198-1) over the edge tuple:

In this experiment every external sender was a non-member with no token, so the check routed traffic exactly as a membership check would. Receiver binding, relation binding and freshness are design rationale and were not tested; the implemented verifier did not check the step label.
policy · noneTrust the claimed relation

Invalid traffic lands in the positive-relationship inbox. This models systems that pass sender and role fields to the prompt without cryptographic enforcement.

policy · downgradeKeep content, remove privilege

The message stays readable in its own section, headed "These messages failed relationship verification". This bundles three changes: the positive label is removed, a provenance notice is added, and the messages move position. The design does not separate them.

policy · dropClosed ingress

Invalid traffic is discarded before prompt construction, so a drop prompt is the control prompt in a different order. It serves as a manipulation check and an upper bound. In an open system it would also exclude benign unknown senders; that cost is not measured.

03 · Frozen confirmatory design

Two deployments, 20 complete blocks

DeploymentsQwen3.5-27B (Alibaba Cloud Model Studio, official) · Mistral Small 4 mistral-small-2603 (Mistral API)
Task18 held-out, text-only binary math questions; labels balanced 9 / 9
Collective8 honest agents, signed symmetric graph, 14 undirected edges (substrate inherited from Physics of Agents)
Dynamics2 synchronous updates, 3 opinion samples per agent and state, T = 0.7
Budgets0, 1, 2, 4 external senders, each targeting exactly 4 agents
Policiesnone · downgrade · drop
Block1 control + 3 budgets × 3 policies = 10 cells × 18 questions = 180 episodes
Sample10 seeds × 2 deployments = 20 blocks, 3,600 episodes

Outcome. The primary outcome is an incorrect terminal majority among the eight honest agents. External senders never vote, so the attacker cannot win by adding its own ballots. It has to flip honest agents.

Model. Mixed-effects logistic regression with budget, policy, deployment and budget × policy as fixed effects, plus random intercepts for question and seed/graph. The fit is MAP with Laplace covariance, and inference uses Wald intervals.

Identification boundary: the prompt never shows sender identifiers, so the budget manipulates repetition (up to about 3.5 identical copies per receiver) and reach (about 4, 6 and 7.5 of 8 agents exposed at b = 1, 2, 4). An identity-count mechanism is excluded by the prompt format, not merely unidentified.

04 · What happened

Attack success rises under trust, stays lower under downgrade, and returns to control under drop

Observed incorrect-majority rates per cell (n = 180 episodes each). Every policy starts from the shared budget-zero control. Hover a point for its counts.

Under trust, Qwen rises from 16.1% to 26.1% and Mistral from 21.1% to 36.1% at budget 4. Under drop, both stay within 2.8 points of control, as the design implies. Downgrade is nearly flat for Qwen and climbs for Mistral while staying below trust. Outcomes are concentrated by question: only 4 of 18 Qwen questions and 10 of 18 Mistral questions ever changed outcome, so the base rates mostly come from a few questions that were wrong in every condition.

05 · Confirmatory tests

H1 and H2 hold in each deployment and pooled

Conditional odds ratios with 95% Wald intervals on a log scale. H1 is the per-sender slope under trust. H2 compares downgrade with trust at the mean positive budget, 7/3. Drop vs trust is shown as a manipulation check. Faded diamonds are the preregistered shared-intercept model.

Within-question randomization tests, which hold the 18 questions fixed and make no assumption about question effects, agree. Paired risk difference for none minus downgrade is 0.028 for Qwen (p = .0017) and 0.048 for Mistral (p = .0043). Deployment-specific magnitudes depend on the random-effect prior because most questions never changed outcome, so read them for direction and rough size.

Why two pooled models. The preregistered model gave both deployments one shared question intercept. Question difficulty, however, runs in opposite directions across the two deployments (correlation −0.49): questions Qwen always failed, Mistral always solved, and vice versa. The shared intercept pulls the pooled estimates outside the range of both deployments. The revised model gives each deployment its own question intercepts. Both models are reported, and the change is listed as a deviation.

Where inference is thin. Qwen's effects come from 4 of its 18 questions. Removing two of them at a time, H2 stays significant in 3 of 6 combinations. With question-clustered inference, the Qwen contrasts are not individually significant. The effects are solid for this question set; generalizing to new questions needs more items.

07 · Takeaway

Keep five layers separate, and let the LLM handle only the last one

The policies do not make the model smarter or detect false math. They change the context a message arrives in. A natural-language or metadata claim should never by itself place a message in a trusted relationship channel. In this study that rule was exercised only against non-members, where checking credentials and checking membership coincide.

1Enrollment

Provision agent identities and the signed graph. Assumed in the experiment.

2Authentication

Verify the token and its edge binding.

3Relationship authorization

Map a verified sign to an allowed route.

4Routing

Apply none, downgrade or drop before the prompt is built.

5Deliberation (LLM)

Solve the task with only the channels the system exposed.

09 · Scope and next steps

What the evidence supports, and what comes next

Supported
  • Trusting self-declared relationships makes the collective vulnerable to more external copies of a false claim, in both deployments.
  • Downgrading that traffic to a labeled unverified channel reduces the effect.
  • Removing it returns behavior to control, so the attack works only through the routed channel.
Not claimed
  • That receiver- or relation-bound credentials beat a membership check; no attacker tried to forge, spoof or replay.
  • An identity-count mechanism; the prompt never shows identities.
  • Which part of downgrade matters: the label, the warning or the position.
  • Benign-traffic utility, a model-family ranking, or a calibrated threshold.
Next decisive experiments
  • An attacker with spoofed member identities, wrong-edge tokens or replayed tokens.
  • A neutral-header downgrade arm in the same position.
  • Benign unknown senders mixed with malicious ones, to measure utility.
  • More and harder questions, plus budgets that bracket the 50% crossing, with deployment-matched couplings.
Why this line of work

The study joins a descriptive model of agent collectives (statistical-mechanics couplings and thresholds) with a security intervention that changes who may exert which kind of influence. In that coupling language, downgrade moves an external edge from the agreeing coupling toward the neutral one. The open question is quantitative: can an admission policy be expressed as a coupling change that predicts where collective failure begins? Answering it needs the matched-coupling and bracketing-budget experiments above.
